# Leverage Playbook

Guy Oseary and Ashton Kutcher put Sound Ventures' office by the front door of Live Nation's headquarters, so every touring artist walked past it on the way in. That is leverage: position, access and relationships doing the work that effort would otherwise do. This skill applies the same mechanics to go-to-market. More clients, more distribution, faster growth.

Fundraising is one possible application. It is never the frame. If a run reads like an investor-targeting exercise, it has gone wrong.

**Core thesis.** A business makes money from what it does. A position makes money from what it's near. The product gets you considered. Leverage gets you chosen. Influence, access and distribution are currency: spend them for ownership and involvement, never for a discount.

## Trigger

The user names a business (theirs, or a new opportunity) and asks for a business plan, GTM strategy, growth strategy, partnership plan or leverage map, or says "run the playbook". Read everything already provided about the business before asking questions.

## Steps

1. **Intake.** Ask only for what's missing: what the business sells, who buys it, current distribution, and the 2 or 3 growth outcomes that matter most in the next 12 months (accounts to win, channels to own, markets to enter). Not the raise.

2. **Inventory the currency.** Before mapping any people, list everything the founder and the business have that isn't the product: equity and upside to give, the existing customer base and distribution, the founder's network, audience and content, industry knowledge, operating credibility. This is what gets spent in every offer. If the list is thin, that is a finding. Say so.

3. **Define the real platform.** What does everyone think this business sells, and what asset does it actually accumulate (customer trust, distribution, data, attention)? Which part should run at deliberately low margin to grow that asset, and what is the high-margin layer on top? Live Nation runs concerts on thin margins because the fans they gather make its sponsorship business worth far more. Output one sentence: "The operating business is X, run at low margin to grow Y. The real business is Z."

4. **Build the leverage map: who makes this business big.** Name 3 to 5 candidates in each category.
   - **Growth partners.** Customers, brands, channel owners and audience owners whose involvement brings their customers and distribution with them. Recruit them as partners with skin in the game (equity or upside), not as accounts to close. Live Nation invested in TURN, then rolled it out across its festivals and venues. One partner, an entire channel.
   - **Door-openers.** For each target account or partner, answer "who do they follow?" The person they admire is what makes the deal wanted, not just the meeting taken.
   - **The leverage hire.** Who already holds the relationships we'd otherwise spend three years building? One hire with the right relationships can be worth more than a sales team.
   - **Position.** Where do the people we need already walk, and how do we sit at their front door (office, partnership, adjacency, stage, content)?

   Strategic investors appear only as a subtype of growth partner: someone whose ownership opens doors. Money alone never qualifies a name for the map. Use web search to find real names. Mark guesses as guesses.

5. **Day-after test.** For every name, ask: what happens the day after they sign that couldn't happen the day before? Cut anyone whose answer is just money or an intro. Rank the survivors by how concrete the doors they open are.

6. **Design the offers.** For each surviving target, write one block: what we give, what we get, what happens on day one. Every "give" comes from the currency inventory, never cash. Equity for distribution we can't buy. Equity, not discounts, for anyone who wants our platform. Leverage hires get equity plus a mandate tied to their relationships. Door-openers get what they actually want: a cause, upside, or the chance to help someone they care about.

7. **Engagement plan.** For the top 5 to 7: the warm path (who we know that they answer), the "who do they follow" answer, what to bring into the room instead of a pitch deck, and the sequence. Land the one name that makes every other conversation easier. First domino first.

8. **Ownership layer.** Identify:
   - one P&L-attack investment: a company that kills a line item on our own P&L, so our own usage guarantees the return;
   - one company our customers already buy from that we'd rather own a piece of;
   - any aggregation play: pooling distribution across companies to earn access no single business gets on its own.

9. **Assemble the GTM plan.** One markdown doc: currency inventory, platform definition, leverage map, ranked targets with offers, engagement sequence, ownership moves, and a 90-day plan:
   - Days 1 to 14: currency inventory, map, cut.
   - Days 15 to 45: warm paths, offers, first meeting with the door-opener in the room.
   - Days 46 to 90: first growth partner signed with skin in the game, or first leverage hire made.

   Frame it as how these people and offers become the distribution strategy. Operator tone: short, actionable, no theory.

## Verification

- The plan reads as GTM. Every target maps to clients, distribution or growth. If any section leads with fundraising, rewrite it.
- Every named target passes the day-after test with a concrete answer. "They'd invest" and "good intro" fail.
- Every offer spends from the currency inventory. Nothing trades access, discounts or distribution for cash when equity or upside was possible.
- The plan names at least one growth partner with skin in the game, one door-opener answer, one leverage-hire profile and one ownership-layer move.
- The doc closes with three questions to re-ask every quarter: Do we have leverage over the deals we want? Is the platform selling something bigger than the business? Do we own stakes in companies we can plug into it?
